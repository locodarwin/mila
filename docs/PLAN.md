## 1. Vision and scope

**In one sentence:** a fast, beautiful project manager that works offline, built for one power user and the people they invite, and that runs anywhere from a single file.

### Goals

1. **Feels instant.** Every interaction is local first, and the network is never in the way.
2. **Feels like yours.** Themes, density, fields, statuses and views all bend to how you work.
3. **You own your data.** Lossless export, readable Markdown, real backups, a one-command database switch, and no third-party calls.
4. **Runs anywhere.** The same build runs on a VPS, a Raspberry Pi, a laptop and, later, the desktop.

### v1 succeeds when

- It is Shawn's only task and project tool, every day.
- It is fully usable on a plane.
- It never loses an edit.
- Someone else can self-host it from the compose file in about five minutes.

### Out of scope

- Open public sign-up and anything enterprise (SAML, enterprise SSO)
- Built-in chat
- Goals/OKRs
- Invoicing or billing
- A plugin marketplace

### Later, deliberately

- **A wiki, in v2** (decided 2026-10-31; it was v2 or v3). Nothing in v1 may block it. Two things keep the door open: the notes editor (Tiptap, stored as Markdown) is built as a reusable component rather than a task-only one, and the sync protocol is generic over entity types, so a `page` entity drops in without protocol changes. (Specified in section 3 on 2026-10-31.)

## 2. Users and tenancy

- **Primary user:** Shawn. Every design trade-off favors one power user first.
- **Secondary users:** collaborators Shawn invites, such as family, clients or a small team.
- **Guests:** see only the specific projects they are given. (Changed 2026-11-02, Shawn, issues #5 and #6: a guest may know the members of the workspace by name and picture, and the other guests they share a project with, so comments name people and a guest can mention them. They still get no email addresses, and no other guests; task history and exports hold to the same rule. The `@` list on a task is whoever can reach it: the members, and the guests its project is shared with.) (M6: a shared workspace's owners and admins give a guest a project, or take it away, from the project's Share button; the guest's open devices pick it up at once, section 7.)
- Sign-up is by invite only. An instance setting can open it later; v1 ships with it closed.
- Personal tasks and team projects both matter. Every user gets a personal workspace and can create or join shared ones.
- The data model is multi-user from the start, even though the first instance has one person on it.
- Personal and shared workspaces use the same model. A personal workspace is a workspace with one member and `is_personal` set, so every feature works in both.
- **Profile pictures:** each person may have a picture, shown wherever their initials were: assignees on rows and cards, comments, mentions, notifications, members, guests and the admin's account list. (Changed before M7: built; this answers the open question in section 15. Settings, Account takes a picture from a file, a drop or a paste and crops it to a circle in the browser, from the keyboard too; without one, a person shows their initials as before. Pictures go to the people who already see the person's name: those who share a live workspace with them, guests included, and the instance's admins. Guests still see "Someone" with initials for people other than notification actors, as with names, and people who left a workspace show initials only. A change of name or picture, and a member joining or leaving, reaches the others' open apps at once. The sidebar footer keeps the Settings gear by the person's name. An admin can take a picture down from Settings, Admin; it is not recorded in the person's security log.)

## 3. Feature set

### v1

#### Structure

- **Workspaces:** each user gets a personal workspace and can create or join shared ones.
- **Projects:** each has an icon, color, description, and can be archived.
- **Tasks with subtasks:** subtasks are real tasks. The model allows unlimited nesting; the UI shows two levels.
- **Checklists:** lightweight checkbox lines inside a task, for things too small to be a subtask.
- **Inbox:** tasks with no project. Quick capture always lands here.
- **Labels:** shared across the whole workspace, so one label can cross projects.

#### Task fields

- Title and notes (Markdown, edited in a rich-text editor)
- Status
- Priority: none, low, medium, high, urgent
- Start date, due date, and an optional time
- Assignees (more than one allowed)
- Labels and custom fields

#### Statuses

- Each project sets its own statuses, and every status belongs to a fixed category: backlog, todo, in progress, done or canceled.
- The categories let views that span projects ("everything in progress") work even though status names differ per project.

#### Views

- List, kanban and calendar.
- Any view can be saved with its own filters, sort, grouping and visible fields.
- Saved views can be personal or shared with the workspace.

#### Personal focus

- **My Day:** a daily list you pull tasks into.
- **Upcoming:** a cross-project view of what is due soon.

#### Collaboration

- Comments, with @mentions arriving alongside notifications in M5
- Activity history on every task
- File attachments
- Invites with four roles: owner, admin, member, guest

#### Time

- Recurring tasks, either on a fixed schedule or a set time after completion.
- Reminders delivered in the app, by web push and by email.

#### Speed

- Command palette (⌘K) that runs every action
- Keyboard shortcuts for everything (Changed before M7: by default; every key can be changed in Settings, Keyboard. See section 4.)
- Instant search that also works offline
- Help: searchable topics and a shortcut reference that shows each person's current keys. (Changed before M7: added. `/help` has a search field, the most used keys and short topics: quick add, views and filters, My Day and Upcoming, repeating tasks, reminders and notifications, offline and sync, touch gestures, and themes (added with custom themes). `/help/shortcuts` is every command with the person's keys, the default beside a changed one and "Off" for a single key while single-key shortcuts are off, plus a note on where the default keys come from. Topics are Svelte components loaded only when read, so they cost no other page and work offline once the service worker has them. The command palette finds help too, loading its index on the first query. Small help links sit where the rules are not on screen: quick add's Syntax legend, a task's repeat and reminders, and Settings, Notifications. Signed-out visitors have no help; it lives inside the app.)
- Themes of one's own, editing every look token, shared as files. (Changed before M7: moved here from After v1; see section 4.)
- Natural-language quick add (Q from anywhere, by default; every key can be changed in Settings, Keyboard): `Call Dana tomorrow 3pm #work !high @shawn` sets date, project, priority and assignee as you type, highlighting each part. It is small to build and most of what makes capture feel fast.

#### Customization

See section 4.

#### Platform

- Installable PWA that works offline
- Realtime sync across tabs and devices
- JSON/CSV import and export
- iCal feeds

## 5. Look and feel

### Principles

1. **Calm by default.** Neutral surfaces, with color used only where it means something: status, priority, labels and the accent.
2. **Content first.** The interface stays quiet so the work stands out.
3. **Keyboard first, touch friendly.** Everything is reachable without a mouse, and nothing is fiddly on a phone.
4. **Motion with a purpose.** Animation explains what changed; it never decorates.
5. **Everything can be undone.** Destructive and bulk actions show an undo toast instead of a confirmation dialog.

### Token system

Three layers:

- **Primitives:** color ramps in OKLCH, spacing scale, corner radii, shadows, font families, motion curves.
- **Semantic tokens:** `surface-0…3`, `text-primary`, `text-secondary`, `text-muted`, `border`, `accent`, `accent-contrast`, `danger`, `status-<category>`, `priority-<level>`. **Themes override this layer.**
- **Component tokens:** only where needed, such as card radius and row height.

**Custom accent:** the chosen hue becomes a full OKLCH ramp. Text on the accent is checked against WCAG AA (4.5:1); if it fails, lightness is nudged until it passes.

(Changed in M6: the "ramp" is the derived tokens the app uses, `--accent`, `--accent-hover`, `--accent-soft`, `--accent-text` and `--accent-contrast`, not a numbered 50 to 950 scale, which nothing would use. The accent as text is worked out for each theme so it reads on all four of that theme's surfaces and on `--accent-soft`, and High contrast holds both the accent and its text to AAA, 7:1. The pre-paint script applies the worked-out accent, so a custom hue shows from the first frame.)

Label and project colors are stored as palette names, not hex values: about twelve tuned hues. Each theme renders them correctly, so a "red" label never turns muddy in dark mode.

Styling is plain CSS with custom properties and Svelte's scoped styles, not Tailwind. Because a theme is just a set of tokens, user themes and accent colors come for free. (Changed before M7: `tokens.css` is still the only place the built-in themes are defined, and every rule in it is keyed on the theme attributes (`[data-theme]`, `[data-scheme]`, `[data-density]`, `[data-font-size]`) rather than on `:root`, so any element that carries all four works out the whole set for itself, derived tokens included: a theme preview is a container with those attributes and the theme's values inline. The registry, `shared/theme-tokens.json`, lists every token a theme may set, with its group, type, layer, bounds, label and the token it defaults to; the server's table matches it and a test holds every custom property the code reads to it. Component tokens (sidebar, rows, cards, board, buttons, fields, checkboxes, menus, panels, dialogs, toasts, the tab bar, the focus ring and the scrims) each default to a semantic token of a lower layer. Text sizes are theme steps (`--type-*`) that the font size preference multiplies, and density's factor and the row height are tokens too, so a theme never breaks the S/M/L and dense choices. A custom theme is drawn from one stylesheet built by `web/src/lib/theme/css.ts` from numbers, registry names and fixed strings only, kept for the pre-paint script so it shows from the first frame; a device's first load shows the base for a frame until the theme arrives. Calm's muted text in light is a shade darker, High contrast's light danger color is darker and its dark accent lightens on hover, so every built-in clears every contrast pair the editor checks.)

### Themes

A theme sets more than colors: also radii, shadows, fonts and a motion profile. There are four themes, and each comes in a light and a dark scheme. The scheme can be fixed or follow the OS. (Changed during M0: the first draft listed Light and Dark as themes of their own, which left Calm and Playful without a dark mode.)

| Theme | Character |
|---|---|
| Standard | Crisp neutral grays, one accent color |
| Calm | Warm paper tones, low-contrast surfaces, serif headings, slower and softer motion |

Scheme: light, dark, or System, which follows the OS setting.

## 6. Data model

Offline support drives three choices:

- **Client-generated IDs:** UUIDv7 (time-ordered), so tasks created offline never collide.
- **Tombstones instead of deletes:** every row has `deleted_at`, so deletions can sync.
- **Field-level last-writer-wins:** each field carries a hybrid logical clock (HLC), stored per row in a `_clocks` JSON column. Two people editing different fields of the same task both win; only edits to the same field conflict.

Manual ordering uses fractional index keys (`sort_key` strings), so dragging a card changes one row instead of renumbering a list.

### Sync and history tables

- **`change_log`:** per-workspace sequence number, entity, id, field, value, HLC, actor. Clients ask for "changes since N". Compacted after 30 days. (M6: the hourly upkeep deletes what was logged more than 30 days ago and raises the workspace's `log_floor` to the last number deleted, in one transaction per workspace. The IDs of applied ops, kept so a retried push changes nothing twice, go after 90 days; an op sent again after that still changes nothing where its row is as it left it, since the merge only takes newer clocks.)
- **`activity`:** the human-readable history ("Shawn moved this to Done"). Derived from changes as they are applied and kept forever. Separate from `change_log` so compaction never erases history.

### Decisions

- **Custom field values get their own table**, not a JSON column on `task`. Field-level sync and indexed filtering are both simpler.
- **Recurrence creates the next task only when the current one is completed.** No pre-generated future copies; the calendar shows future occurrences as ghost entries. The client creates the next task itself with a **deterministic ID** (a hash of the completed task alone), so recurrence works offline and two devices completing the same task offline merge instead of duplicating. The hash leaves out the next date because that depends on the day and timezone of the completion, so two devices completing the same overdue task on different days would make two copies; the date merges like any other field. It takes the completed task rather than the series because a series ID can name the task being completed itself, which would end the series.
- **A reminder relative to the due date stores its offset, and the server works out its moment.** It fires at the task's due moment (its due time, or 09:00 for an all-day task, in the person's timezone) less the offset, whatever remind_at says, so it follows the task when anyone reschedules it, including people who cannot see the reminder. The app keeps remind_at in step for its own edits, for display. The next task of a repeating series gets the relative reminders of the task it follows: the completing device copies its user's own, and the server copies everyone else's under the same derived IDs. Reminders and due notices more than 12 hours late are let go, and a due date set after its moment has passed is not announced.
- **A push subscription belongs to the session that made it.** Pushes carry task titles and comment text, so a browser that signs out, or whose session is revoked from another device or expires, stops getting them at once, before the app on it can clean up. The app sends its subscription again at every start, which ties it to the current session and to the current key, and it unsubscribes the browser when it signs out, when it starts and finds itself signed out, and when someone other than the person who turned push on signs in. A push is queued once per device, so a push service that is down is retried without repeating the message on the others; one that says the subscription is gone (404 or 410) has it deleted. A queued push names the account and session it was for, and is dropped if the browser has been signed in again since. (Added 2026-11-02, Shawn: three days before a session ends, its browser gets one push with no task content, "Open Mila to keep notifications on this device", or, near the 90-day limit, "Sign in to Mila again"; `warned_at` keeps it to once for each time an end comes near. The rule itself stays.)
- **A calendar feed is worked out again at every fetch, as its owner sees the workspace then.** It reads the same rows sync would send them, so a guest's feed holds only shared projects, and leaving the workspace, losing a project or a deleted view ends it (404). Tasks with a due date become events: all day, or 30 minutes at the due time in UTC. A schedule-mode repeating task carries its RRULE; one with a time is written in its owner's time zone (`TZID` and a `VTIMEZONE` built from Go's zone data) rather than UTC, because a rule repeated in UTC drifts an hour across daylight saving and lands on the wrong weekday when UTC is already on the next day. An event's DTSTAMP and LAST-MODIFIED are when the task last changed, by its clocks, so an unchanged feed is byte for byte the same and its ETag answers 304. An undated task in My Day is on its My Day date, or today while it is carried over.
- **Push services and the mail server are other people's servers, so their work does not wait in line with the rest.** Jobs run one at a time, except push sends, which run up to eight at once beside them, each limited to 10 seconds, and emails, two of each kind at a time in a lane of their own. A push service that does not answer is left alone for two minutes, its pushes failing at once and being retried later, so one that hangs, by fault or by design, delays no reminder, email or digest and costs the other pushes little. A mail server that does not answer delays only email. The periodic work (the reminder and due scan, the digest, upkeep) runs in a loop of its own, so no run of jobs holds it up.
- **A reference inside a view's JSON is a bare ID in a value position** (`{"by": "field", "field": "<id>"}`, `"show": ["<id>"]`), never an object key or a prefixed string. A restore with fresh IDs rewrites string values it knows, so such references follow the field, label, person or project to its new ID; a key or a prefixed ID would silently point at nothing. (Changed before M7: written down with the view display.)
- **Notes conflict per whole field in v1.** If two people edit the same notes offline, the later save wins and the earlier version stays in activity history. Live merging (Yjs) can come later without a schema change. (Changed in v2: notes merge live, section 7. The notes column stays, as the document rendered to Markdown, so the schema change was a table added, `doc_updates`, not one changed.)

## 7. Architecture, sync, offline and realtime

### Shape

One process, with no extra infrastructure: no Redis, no queue, no separate worker.

```text
 browser (PWA)                         single Go binary
┌───────────────────────┐   HTTPS   ┌──────────────────────────────┐
│ Svelte UI             │◄─────────►│ static files (embedded web/) │
│ in-memory store       │  REST     │ auth · invites · files · iCal│
│ IndexedDB persistence │  sync     │ sync push/pull               │
│ outbox (pending ops)  │◄═════════►│ WebSocket hub (pokes)        │
│ service worker        │    WS     │ job runner (reminders, mail, │
└───────────────────────┘           │   push, backups, compaction) │
                                    └──────────────┬───────────────┘
                                         SQLite file  or  Postgres
```

Scaling past one instance later uses Postgres `LISTEN/NOTIFY` to fan out pokes. The cost of one process is that scaling out requires Postgres; the gain is that every deployment is "run one file".

### Repository layout

```text
server/     Go: cmd/mila, internal/{config,db,store,httpapi,webui,auth,sync,domain,jobs,mail,push,ical,files}
web/        SvelteKit 3 (static adapter, SPA mode), Svelte 5, TypeScript
shared/     openapi.yaml, filter-spec test vectors, op schemas (JSON) used by both sides
deploy/     compose.yaml, Caddyfile, systemd unit (the Dockerfile is at the root)
docs/       PLAN.md and design notes
```

### Server

- **HTTP:** the Go standard library router (method and path patterns), plus small middleware for auth, CSRF and rate limits.
- **Database drivers:** `modernc.org/sqlite` (pure Go, no cgo, trivial cross-compiling) and `pgx`.
- **Queries:** a portable SQL subset; `sqlc` generates typed code per engine behind one `Store` interface. CI runs the whole suite on both databases. Maintaining two dialects is the price of deployment flexibility, and CI keeps it honest.
- **Migrations:** `goose`, one set per dialect, embedded in the binary.
- **WebSockets:** `coder/websocket`. **Passkeys:** `go-webauthn`.
- **Jobs:** a `jobs` table polled in-process, for reminders, email, web push, nightly backups, log compaction and token cleanup. (Changed in M6: slow work, exports, imports and backups, runs in a slow lane: job kinds starting `slow.`, one at a time beside everything else and held for six hours rather than five minutes, so a large export never delays a reminder and is never taken twice while it runs.)
- **Web push keys** (VAPID) are generated on first run and stored in the database (`instance_setting`). Messages are encrypted for each browser (RFC 8291) and signed (RFC 8292) with the standard library, and sent only to public addresses, since browsers name the endpoints.
- **API:** spec-first OpenAPI (see section 11).
- **Yjs (v2, for the wiki and task notes):** the server reads and writes Yjs data itself with `github.com/reearth/ygo`, a pure-Go port, behind a small interface of Mila's own: merge, state vector, diff, read as ProseMirror JSON, and replace from it. It merges and compacts a document's updates, works out what a device lacks, renders the Markdown for search, export, the API, email and calendar feeds from the merged document rather than trusting a copy a device wrote, and turns a REST write into a Yjs update. (Decided 2026-10-31, from a trial against real Yjs and Tiptap documents: yjs 13.6, `@tiptap/y-tiptap` 3.0, which Tiptap 3's Collaboration uses rather than y-prosemirror, and ygo 1.55.) The port is young, so its version is pinned and CI runs the trial's documents through it: concurrent offline edits merged in Go must read back in JS exactly, and Go's reading must match the editor's own. The fallback, proven in the same trial, is yrs, the Rust port, built for wasm32-wasip1 and run through wazero, which keeps the binary free of cgo at the cost of a Rust build step, about 3.3 MB of binary and about 150 ms of compiling at start.
  - A REST write replaces only the top-level blocks that differ, so an edit a device has not yet sent survives unless it was inside a block the write changed. Comparing blocks needs the editor schema's default attributes, which y-tiptap stores on every node, in a table generated from Tiptap's schema.
  - Every write the server makes uses a fresh Yjs client ID.
  - **How documents sync** (decided 2026-10-31, before the wiki's build). A live document is a task's notes or a wiki page's body, held as the fragment `default` that y-tiptap writes. Its edits travel as rows of a synced entity, `doc_update`: each row holds one Yjs update (version 1, base64) and names its task, later its page, so it follows that row for guests, trash and history like comments. Devices push their edits as new rows, at most one every half second while someone types and at once when the editor closes, and apply the rows they pull to the open document. Rows are never changed, only deleted, so the sync protocol stays as it is: generic over entities, last writer wins per field.
  - The server reads every row it accepts. It renders the document's Markdown and writes it to the task's `notes` (or the page's `body`), stamped by the server, so search, filters, export, the API, email and calendar feeds go on reading plain text, and devices that have not loaded the document still show it. A history entry for the notes is kept per editing session: the same person within five minutes updates their last entry rather than adding one.
  - A write to `notes` that is not a Yjs update (REST, the CSV import, `mila dev generate`, or an app from before this change) is turned by the server into a Yjs update that replaces only the top-level blocks that differ, stored as a `doc_update` row of its own. Markdown is read in Go with goldmark into the structure the editor reads from it, and written out so that the editor reads it back as the same document; `shared/doc-vectors.json` holds both directions to the editor's own answers, as the filter vectors do. (Changed while building it: the first draft said the server would write Markdown as `@tiptap/markdown` writes it, but that writer leaves some text that reads back differently, "# not a heading" as a heading, so the server escapes properly instead and the vectors check what the editor reads back. The few places where the server reads Markdown better than the editor, every entity and every inline HTML mark, are named in the tests.)
  - When a document has more than 32 rows, the server merges them into one row and deletes the rest in the same transaction; devices that pull the delete drop the rows, and the merged row changes nothing they already hold.
  - Existing notes get their first row from the server: a one-time job at start writes a `doc_update` for every task whose notes are not empty and that has none. A device that holds notes with no rows yet shows them read-only until the rows arrive, since a document made from the same Markdown on two devices would come out twice when they merge.
  - Editing a document is left out of the app's undo (Ctrl+Z outside the editor); the editor's own undo, Yjs's, takes back typing, one person's at a time.
  - Sync's schema version goes to 2 with `doc_update`, so open apps from before reload rather than misread rows they do not know.
  - (Built 2026-10-31 for task notes, part 1 of the wiki's build: `server/internal/ydoc` reads, merges, compacts and writes documents and Markdown; `syncer/docs.go` renders notes, turns writes to notes into rows and merges rows; `web/src/lib/sync/docs.ts` is a document on the device. Rows merged away are deleted from the database once their delete is logged, and devices drop them as they pull it, rather than keeping them in the trash. Merging waits until a document has 64 rows and the small ones outweigh the largest, which keeps the log's growth to about twice what was typed. Raw rows are not offered by the REST API.)
  - Presence travels over the WebSocket, which until now only spoke to devices: a device names the document it has open and its cursor, the server passes that to the others who can see the document, and nothing of it is stored. (Built 2026-10-31, part 2: devices send Yjs awareness updates, at most one every tenth of a second; `syncer/presence.go` lets a device into a document's room only where it may see the document, stamps every state with the sender's user ID in place of whatever user the device named, refuses a Yjs client ID another connection already uses, gives a device that opens a document the states of those already in it, and announces a device gone when it closes the document, loses access or drops off. Messages are bounded in size and rate, a window holds at most eight documents, and presence a slow device cannot take is dropped rather than closing its socket. Task notes show the others' faces above them and their carets, named, in the text; carets are hidden from screen readers, which hear "Pia is here" instead, and one's own cursor in another window is not drawn.)

### Frontend

- **State:** the whole workspace in memory as reactive Svelte stores, persisted to IndexedDB through Dexie. A few thousand tasks fit easily and every view, filter and drag is instant. SQLite in the browser (WASM) is the fallback if workspaces ever reach tens of thousands of tasks.
- **Search:** MiniSearch in a web worker; works offline. (Changed in M4: the command palette scores titles, notes, projects, views and commands in memory instead. The whole workspace is already in memory and a few thousand tasks search instantly; MiniSearch returns if typo tolerance or large workspaces call for it.)
- **Filters:** one JSON filter spec implemented in TypeScript and Go. Shared test vectors in `shared/` keep them in agreement. The server needs its copy for iCal feeds, the API and reminders. (M4 built the TypeScript half, `web/src/lib/filter.ts`. M5 added the Go half, `server/internal/filter`, for calendar feeds of a saved view, and `shared/filter-vectors.json`, which both pass. They agree down to how text is trimmed and lowercased: Go follows JavaScript's rules for the dotted capital I and a word-final sigma.) (Changed before M7: both copies read conditions on custom fields, in the same order of rules: a condition on a field the viewer does not know is read as if no task had a value, so a guest sees a shared view's tasks within their projects and never more; a malformed one matches nothing; one with nothing chosen yet is ignored. Keys are read exactly as written in both, so Go does not take "Text" for "text" as encoding/json would, and a field of a type the app does not know matches nothing. The vectors hold 192 match and 15 count cases.)
- **Editor:** Tiptap (ProseMirror), stored as Markdown, built as a reusable component (the wiki will need it).
- **Drag and drop:** pragmatic-drag-and-drop, for kanban, lists, the calendar and the sidebar. (Changed in M4: the board and the calendar use the browser's own drag and drop, with Alt and the arrow keys doing the same moves from the keyboard; lists reorder with Alt and the arrows. Native drag and drop does nothing on touch screens, so on phones a card moves by changing its status in the task panel. Revisit with the library if touch dragging is wanted.)
- **Styling:** plain CSS design tokens (section 5).
- **Motion:** Svelte transitions, the Web Animations API for the page between views (M6, section 5), FLIP for reordering.
- **Service worker:** caches the app shell; shows "Update available" when a new build is deployed. (M6: the new service worker takes over at once, so the page offers a reload, in a toast that stays until it is used or closed, when a new service worker takes over a page it already controlled or a lazily loaded part of the old build no longer loads. An open app asks for a new service worker every half hour and when it comes back online.)

### Sync protocol

1. **Bootstrap.** On first load the client streams a snapshot of every workspace it can access, records `lastSeq` for each, and persists everything locally. (Changed in M6: the snapshot holds the trash too, rows deleted but not yet purged and the rows hanging off a deleted task. The change log carries only the fields an op changed, so a device that bootstrapped without a deleted task turned a later restore of it into a task with no title, and its checklist and comments never came back; the long run of the convergence fuzz test found it through the guest, whose resets bootstrap often. A comment, checklist item or attachment deleted on its own comes too, text included, to people who join after it was deleted, for the 30 days it is kept: undoing the delete would otherwise bring back a row with no text. That shows them nothing new, since the task's history already holds what the row said. The server also writes the snapshot as it reads it, a row at a time, rather than holding it whole: a 5,000-task workspace's is about 8 MB of JSON, 1.1 MB gzipped.)
2. **Making a change.** Every edit becomes an operation: `{opId, entity, id, fields: {name: value}, hlc}`. It is saved to IndexedDB with its row in one transaction, then shown, so a reload or a closed tab never loses it. Edits run one at a time, and fields that depend on other rows (a new task's sort key) are worked out when the edit runs, after earlier edits have landed. **An edit is in the outbox before the UI treats it as done.** (Changed 2026-11-01: an IndexedDB write takes a moment, and a page that reloads or closes in it aborts the write, so an edit that had already shown, such as a delete with its Undo toast, could be lost. An edit is now kept first in a journal in localStorage, which is written at once (`web/src/lib/sync/journal.ts`), then shown, then written to IndexedDB in the order edits were made, and cleared from the journal; the engine puts anything left in the journal into IndexedDB when it next starts, merging each field by its clock. An edit kept twice is harmless, since the server takes an op once by its opId. Fields that depend on other rows see every earlier edit, because each edit shows as it is made. A caller's await ends once the edit is kept; if the journal cannot keep it (storage full), once IndexedDB has it, as before.)
3. **Push.** The outbox is sent in batches over HTTP. (Changed in M2: the first draft sent ops over the WebSocket with HTTP as a fallback; one path is simpler, and the socket carries only pokes.) For each op the server checks permission, validates the schema, compares the incoming HLC with the field's stored HLC, and writes the row plus its `change_log` entry in one transaction, numbered by the workspace's own counter so commit order is change order. Ops are idempotent by `opId`, so retries are harmless. A rejected op comes back with the server's copy of the row, and the client reverts every field the op was the last local write to.
4. **Realtime.** After each commit the server sends a poke `{workspace, seq}` to connected clients, which pull changes since their `lastSeq`. Live updates, reconnecting and multiple tabs all use this one path. Each tab keeps its own socket, and a Web Lock lets only one tab send the outbox at a time. (Changed in M2 from a BroadcastChannel leader tab, which would have been more machinery for the same result.)
5. **Opening offline.** The service worker serves the app shell, and the last known user and workspaces are kept on the device, so the app starts with no network at all. Signing out deletes both, with every workspace's local copy. (Changed before M7: each workspace's people, members and those who left, are kept on the device too (`mila.people`), so an offline start still knows who is who. People's pictures use the browser's HTTP cache, not the service worker: each is served under a random key that is never reused, `private, max-age` a year and `immutable`, so a picture seen once shows offline for a year and one never seen shows initials. Signing out, deleting the account and finding the session gone answer with `Clear-Site-Data: "cache"`, which empties that HTTP cache (pictures and attachments) and leaves Cache Storage, the app shell, alone. Browsers honor it only over HTTPS or on localhost, so a plain-HTTP self-host keeps what it cached, reachable only by its 128-bit URL.)
6. **Compaction.** A client whose `lastSeq` falls behind the compaction horizon re-bootstraps. (M6: a pull from below the workspace's `log_floor` answers 410, and the app bootstraps again. It sends its outbox before it pulls, and replays what is still unsent on top of a new copy, so a device offline for longer than 30 days keeps its edits. A pull also carries the sync epoch its copy came with: bootstrap hands out the instance's epoch, a restore from a backup makes a new one, and a pull with any other epoch, or none, answers 410, because a restored database hands out change numbers a device may already have passed.)
7. **Permissions.** The change log is filtered by project access, so guests only receive their projects. Revoking access sends a reset, which wipes and re-bootstraps that workspace on the affected devices. (M6: giving access resets too, since the rows a guest now sees were never sent to them. A reset raises the workspace's change number and records it on the membership (`reset_seq`, `store.ResetMember`); a pull from before it answers 410, so the device bootstraps again, keeping and replaying its unsent edits, and a device that was offline at the time hears of it at its next pull. The socket's `{"type":"reset"}` only makes the open ones pull at once. Joining a workspace resets the new member too, since a device of someone who was a member before may never have heard they left and still hold what they saw then. Changing a member's role to or from guest resets them (a guest who moves up loses the projects given to them one by one, so made a guest again they start with none), and so does moving a row between two projects for every guest who sees one and not the other: the change log holds only the fields an op changed, filed under the project the row was in then, so a guest who gains the row would get a few fields of it and none of its comments, and one who loses it would keep a stale copy. The convergence fuzz test has a guest who is given projects and loses them while every device edits, and it fails with either reset turned off.) (Changed in M6, found by the long fuzz run: an edit by a guest to a row they no longer see is refused even when it lost to a newer one and would have changed nothing. Taken as applied, it left the guest's device holding the row, rebuilt from its unsent edits, with nothing to correct it.)
8. **Clock safety.** The server rejects HLCs more than about 60 seconds in the future, so a device with a wrong clock cannot win every conflict forever. (Changed before M7: a write through the REST endpoints (section 11) enters the same merge as one op, stamped by the server rather than a device: now, or one tick after the newest clock among the fields it writes, whichever is later, with the node `api`. A REST write therefore never silently loses to a write the server already holds, even from a device up to a minute ahead, while a skewed clock on one field does not push the rest of the row's stamps ahead. It is checked against its row under a row lock (`FOR UPDATE` on Postgres; SQLite's writes take turns already), so an `If-Match` precondition holds when the write lands and two writes in one millisecond still order. A create fills in every field it was not given (the registry's `def`, which equals the migrations' DEFAULTs and `DEFAULTS` in entities.ts), so devices get whole rows from the change log. A write that sets a field to what it holds already is still stamped and logged, as the newest word on it, so an older edit a device made offline cannot undo it when it arrives later; it only makes no entry in the task's history and tells the watcher nothing. Deleting what is deleted already keeps its time and is stamped the same way, and a comment whose body changes through REST gets `edited_at`, as the app sets it. Logged, poked, notified, put in history and reset for guests exactly as a push is. The convergence fuzz test has a REST writer beside its devices, on the simulated clock and a little behind them, aimed at fields a device just wrote; it fails when the stamp stops observing.)
9. **Version gate.** Every op carries the client's schema version. Too old gets "update required", and the service worker updates. (Changed in M6: the version travels once per push rather than on every op, as `schema`, `syncer.Schema` on the server and `SYNC_SCHEMA` in `entities.ts`, raised whenever ops from an older app would be refused or misread; a test fails when the synced fields change, as the reminder to decide. A push of any other version is refused whole before any op is applied, with 426 and `update_required`. A push with no version comes from an app older than the gate (M5), which cannot show the toast; it counts as version 1, the shape those ops have, so an M5 tab left open through the upgrade keeps syncing its edits. Such a tab also sends no epoch, so each change it hears of costs it a full bootstrap until it is reloaded. The app stops sending and pulling, keeps its outbox, and shows a toast with a Reload button that stays until it is used, and the sync indicator says an update is needed and how many edits are waiting; the reloaded app sends the outbox. Each outbox entry keeps the version it was made under, and the app rewrites older ones into its own shape before sending, one version step at a time, so kept edits are not misread as the new version. Any other version rather than only older ones, since the server serves the app: reloading always brings the matching one.)
10. **Generic over entities.** Nothing in the protocol is task-specific; new entity types (wiki pages, for one) are a schema addition, not a protocol change.

**Not synced to devices:** attachment files (fetched on demand, then cached) and full activity history (paged in while online). Comments do sync, so they can be read offline.

(2026-11-07: text never holds a NUL, which Postgres can't store and nothing shows. Sync and REST refuse one in any text field ("must not hold a NUL character"); the app takes them out before an edit is kept or sent (`Replica.prepare`); a restore takes them out of an export made on SQLite; and the server's Markdown of a live document leaves them out. 2026-11-08, Shawn: "fix now": the rest of the server's text too. Every other endpoint refuses a NUL at the door, 400 "Text can't hold a NUL character.": in the address and query (`refuseNULs`), and in the JSON bodies read by `decode`, the actions, the row endpoints and OAuth's registration and token form (`escapesNUL`, `httpapi/refusenul.go`; not `nul.go`, a name Windows reserves). Sync's push still refuses one op by op, so an old device's outbox isn't stuck behind one. Names (accounts and workspaces) refuse one in `cleanName` too, for any way in besides the API.)

## 9. Auth and security

### Sign-in without passwords

- Passkeys are the primary method.
- Email magic links are the backup: 15-minute expiry, single use, only a hash stored.
- GitHub/Google sign-in is optional, enabled by configuration. It links to an existing account only when the provider confirms the email is verified. (Decided for M7: not in v1. Passkeys and email links cover sign-in, and adding providers later changes nothing that exists.)
- First run prints a one-time setup link to the log, which creates the instance owner.
- (Added for M7: a person can change their email address in Settings, Account. Until now the address chosen at setup or in an invitation could not be changed, and a mistyped one could only be fixed in the database. The new address takes over when its owner opens a link sent to it, so a typo cannot lock anyone out; the old address is told, and the change goes in the security log, so a stolen session cannot quietly move the account's sign-in elsewhere. Built: `email_changes` (migration 00018) keeps the hash of each link's token; a link works once, for 24 hours, and only the newest one a person asked for, at most five an hour. An address another account uses is refused, when asked and again when the link is opened. The link's page spends the token by a request, not on opening, as the sign-in link's does, so mail scanners cannot use it up, and it needs no session. Sign-in links still open for the old address stop working, the security log shows the change from one address to the other, the old address gets an email through the mail lane, and members' open apps see the new address at once. Deleting the account drops its pending changes. (Changed in the M7 security review: a change works only while the session that asked for it lasts, `email_changes.session_id` from migration 00039, so signing out, revoking that session from Settings, Security, or an admin's sign out everywhere ends it.))
- Without SMTP, magic links fall back to the server log, and `mila user login-link <email>` prints a sign-in link, so an admin can never be locked out.

### Sessions

- Server-side sessions; the browser holds only a random token in an HttpOnly, Secure, SameSite=Lax cookie.
- 30-day sliding lifetime. (Changed in the M7 security review: never past 90 days from signing in, however often it is used, so a stolen cookie used now and then does not last for good.)
- Settings → Devices lists sessions and revokes any of them.
- A revoked or signed-out device wipes its local data (IndexedDB and caches) on its next connection; signing out on the device wipes immediately. This matters because offline mode keeps a full copy of the data on each device.
- WebSocket connections authenticate with the same cookie and have their origin checked.
- (Added in the M7 security review: what would let a stolen session outlast its theft needs a sign-in from the last 10 minutes: adding a passkey, asking to change the email address, deleting the account. Removing a passkey too, since the 2026-11-05 review: without it, an email link could take away the passkeys that made it not enough. An older session gets 403 with the code `sign_in_again`, and the app offers Sign in again, which opens the sign-in page with the address filled in and comes back afterward, by an email link too, whose page is remembered for the 15 minutes the link works. A sign-in on a browser that still holds a session ends that session, so signing in again leaves one per browser. This replaces the typed address as the only check before deleting an account, deferred from M6.)

### Authorization

- Two layers: instance admin (users, invites, instance settings) and workspace roles (owner, admin, member, guest).
- One function, `can(actor, action, entity)`, in the domain layer, used by REST, sync and jobs alike, so no path can skip it.
- Table-driven tests cover the whole permission matrix.

(M6: instance admins are `users.is_instance_admin`, and their actions are `authz.InstanceAction`s, checked by `authz.CanInstance` against the flag as it stands in the database at each request. Admins see Settings, Admin: the instance's version, database, email mode, passkeys, address and backups (with "Back up now"), every account with when it was last active and how many devices are signed in, every open invitation in every workspace, which they can cancel, and they can make someone an admin, take it away, or sign someone out everywhere. (Changed in the M7 security review: signing out everywhere ends, besides sessions, tokens, feeds and email-to-task addresses, the person's pending email changes and unused sign-in links and removes every passkey, so someone who took the account cannot come back by one they asked for or added; the person is emailed and signs in again by an email link.) The instance always keeps one admin; the check locks the admins' rows first, so two admins stepping down at once on Postgres cannot leave none. `mila user make-admin <email>` at the server is the rescue when no admin is left who can. Sharing a project with a guest is the workspace action `projects.share`, for owners and admins. Deferred in M6: opening sign-up (v1 ships invite-only, section 2), invitations to the instance without a workspace (`invites.workspace_id` would have to become nullable, a table rebuild on SQLite), suspending accounts (signing out everywhere covers the urgent case), and the optional Prometheus `/metrics` (section 8).)

### Hardening

- **CSRF:** SameSite cookies plus an Origin check on every state-changing request.
- **Content Security Policy:** no inline scripts, nothing from third parties, `frame-ancestors 'none'`. HSTS via Caddy (or the binary's own TLS).
- Rate limits (in-memory token buckets): sign-in endpoints per IP and per account; magic-link emails capped at 5 per hour per address. (Changed before M7: the REST entity endpoints are limited per user, reads to 1,200 a minute and writes to 300, each after a burst of twice as many, answering 429 with `Retry-After: 60`. The app never calls them and sync is not limited, so these only bound a runaway script. They are fields of the server's dependencies, not settings; a setting can come with API tokens.) (M6: per account, the cap of 5 sign-in emails an hour is the limit; a passkey names no account until it is checked, and a per-account bucket after that waits for the M7 security review. The admin endpoints are under the same Origin check as every API write, and a test sends each of them from another origin and as someone who is not an admin.)
- **Notes:** Markdown sanitized on the client with DOMPurify. The server generates HTML only for email. (Changed in the M7 security review, to say what is built: notes are Markdown that the editor, TipTap, parses into its own schema, so HTML in them becomes text; nothing in the app renders user content as HTML (no `{@html}`, no `innerHTML`), and email is plain text. DOMPurify is not used.)
- **Pictures:** (Changed before M7: added.) The server accepts only a PNG, 4 MB and 16 to 1024 pixels on a side at most, checked from the header before any pixel is decoded, with at most two decodes at once and 30 changes an hour per account after a burst of 10, removals included (renames have a limit of their own, the same size, since each tells everyone the person shares a workspace with; a change to the same value tells nobody); the app draws the cropped picture as a PNG of at most 512 pixels, so any format the browser can show works there. The server crops the middle square, scales it to 256 pixels at most and encodes it again (JPEG at quality 85 when opaque, PNG when it has transparency), which drops EXIF, location and every other chunk. It is stored under a random key and served at `/api/v1/avatars/<key>` only to its owner, people who share a live workspace with them and instance admins; anyone else gets 404, as for a key that names nothing. The answer carries `default-src 'none'; sandbox`, `nosniff` and `Cross-Origin-Resource-Policy: same-origin`, and only JPEG or PNG is ever served. A replaced or removed picture's URL is 404 at once, and the hourly sweep deletes the file.
- **Attachments:** served only to signed-in members who can see the task, under a sandboxing CSP; PNG, JPEG, GIF and WebP display inline (never SVG), everything else downloads; `nosniff`; 25 MB limit. (Changed in M3 from expiring signed URLs: the session cookie already proves who is asking, and S3 storage is not built yet. Signed URLs return if attachments move to S3.)
- **iCal tokens:** unguessable, read-only, scoped to a user, project or view, revocable.

### Security review (M7)

Three read-only reviews of the code before launch, one each for sign-in and sessions, for who may see and change what, and for input, outbound requests and hardening, found nothing critical. What they found, and what changed, 2026-10-30:

- **A stolen session could become the account for good.** Holding a session was enough to add a passkey or move the email address. Those, deleting the account and making a calendar feed (which outlives sessions) now need a sign-in from the last 10 minutes (section 9, Sessions), and for an account with a passkey that sign-in must be a passkey: an email link proves only a look into the mailbox. Sessions record how they were signed in (`sessions.method`, migration 00019). Adding a passkey emails the person, unless it is a new account's first, in its first hour. An admin's "sign out everywhere" ends the person's calendar feeds too.
- **Sign-in links told who has an account** by how long the answer took, since the email went out first. The server now answers and then sends. A sign-in link for another account than the one a browser is signed in to asks before switching (`POST /auth/magic-link/peek` reads a link's address without spending it), so a link someone sends cannot quietly sign a person in as them. (Changed in the M7 security review: switching deletes the first person's copy on the device, unsent edits included, as signing out does, and the page asking says how many would go.)
- **Rate limits.** Invitations: twenty, then two an hour, per person (`MILA_INVITE_RATE` sets the hourly number, 2 by default, the burst being ten times it), and fifty waiting per workspace, since each is an email with words its sender chose. Asking for a new email address: five an hour, refused requests included, so it cannot list who has an account. Uploads: thirty, then a hundred an hour, per person. Limits per address count an IPv6 address by its /64. A request through a proxy `MILA_TRUSTED_PROXIES` does not name is logged once, since its visitors would all share one sign-in limit.
- **Invitations outlived their sender's rights.** Accepting one now checks that whoever sent it is still a member who may invite at that role, so it is no way back in for someone removed or demoted.
- **Access that ended stayed readable.** A comment's text was kept in its task's history for good, for everyone who sees the task, though the comment was deleted; history now leaves it out, and entries written before are scrubbed when read, exported or imported. Leaving a workspace deletes the notifications about it, a guest losing a project those about its tasks, and the list hides any others about what the person no longer sees. A finished export downloads only while its maker is a member. Each sync write reads the person's membership again inside its transaction, so a push under way stops when they are removed, demoted or given fewer projects.
- **Guests.** Task history and a guest's export name people as the guest may know them (section 2), and say "Someone" for another guest they share no project with. A guest is still told that something changed in the workspace when it is in a project they cannot see; what changed never reaches them, only when, and that is accepted.
- **Rows the app derives.** A new pair row (a label on a task, an assignee, a task in someone's day, a field's value) must have the ID the pair makes, so a row given another cannot stand in its way; a field's value must be on a task in the field's project, unless the field is workspace-wide.
- **Push.** A browser gives the server an address to send to; on a home server another kind of address could reach into the house. Mila sends only over HTTPS on port 443 to the push services browsers use (Google, Mozilla, Apple, Microsoft), and the connection is refused to private and reserved addresses besides, NAT64's among them.
- **Disk and memory.** Uploads and imports are refused when less than 1 GB is free. An import's upload is held to one at a time per person from before it is read (two sent together both passed the old check), and one row of an export may take at most 8 MB of JSON, so a small zip cannot fill memory.
- **Slow connections.** A request body must arrive within a minute, or fifteen for an upload; one person may hold at most 20 sync sockets.
- **Deployment.** `deploy/compose.yaml` drops every Linux capability (Caddy keeps binding low ports), forbids new privileges and runs Mila on a read-only root with `/tmp` in memory; Postgres has no default password. The staging deploy no longer copies Mila's log, which can hold sign-in links, into the workflow's log, and CI's token is read-only unless a job needs more. A malformed `MILA_SMTP_URL` no longer prints its password in the error.

### Security review (2026-11-05)

Six read-only reviews of the whole of Mila as it stood at `373b3bc` (sign-in and sessions; who may see and change what; script injection and the app; files, imports and resources; webhooks, email, tokens and automations; the desktop app, deployment and CI) found nothing critical and no way to run script. What they found, and what changed:

- **One person could take the server down with a document.** Writing notes or a page body as Markdown worked out what the old and new documents share with a table of their two block counts multiplied, gigabytes for a long page; now the blocks both start and end with are set aside first, and past four million cells the middle is replaced whole. Reading and writing Markdown took time of the text's length squared for many shapes of text (a line of thousands of `>`, a paragraph of thousands of `[a](` or plain lines, unclosed column and fold markers, link definitions), minutes for a page, inside the write's transaction, which every other write waits for. Each is now in line with its length (`TestMarkdownTakesLinearTime`, every case at a page body's 1,000,000 characters). What reads differently, only for text nobody writes: one line opens at most 64 quotes and list items, the rest of it being text; a document is written as Markdown 200 levels deep at most, deeper blocks becoming a paragraph of their text; past 64 failed tries at a link in a document, a link's address and title are looked for in the next 1 KB of their line only; and link definitions are read from a paragraph's first 1,000 lines.
- **A small import could fill the memory.** A zip of half a megabyte unpacked into 512 MB of JSON and some 4 GB in memory. workspace.json may now unpack to at most 32 times what it takes in the zip (Mila's own exports zip about 8.5 to 1, at least 4 MB), and its rows take at most four times that in memory, reckoned from the JSON as it is read (Mila's own reckon at under six times their JSON).
- **An email link could take away the passkeys that made it not enough.** Removing a passkey needed only a session, so someone who could read the mailbox for a moment signed in by link, removed every passkey, and was then let change the address, add their own passkey and make a token; any stolen session could remove them too. Removing a passkey now needs a recent sign-in, with a passkey while the account has one, and the person is emailed that it is gone.
- **An admin could take a webhook with them.** Changing a webhook's address left it its maker's, and only what someone made is paused when they leave, so an admin could point another's webhook at their own server and keep the workspace's work coming after they were removed. Whoever changes its address or makes it a new secret (which would let them sign deliveries of their own) now has it, as turning it back on already did, and whoever had it is told.
- **IDs could be taken in advance from another workspace.** IDs are one space for every workspace, and some are worked out from others: a pair's row (a label on a task, an assignee, a dependency, a task in someone's day, a field's value) took the XOR of its two IDs, which anyone could match with two IDs of their own; and a repeating task's next one and what it copies, or a wiki's main page, could be made first elsewhere under their IDs. Either way the real row could never be made, so a guest or someone who left could keep anyone from labeling or assigning the tasks they knew. A pair's ID is now a hash of the pair (sync schema 5). Migration 38 moves every pair row to its hash (with the change log and task history), keeps each old ID in `pair_ids` so an edit or REST call from before still lands, and changes the sync epoch, so every device fetches a fresh copy once; an app renames the edits it kept before sending them. A new row's ID of a shape Mila works out (a version 8 UUID, or one not of RFC 9562's variant) is accepted from a device or script only where it is the one Mila would work out for that row there, and an import keeping its IDs gives a fresh one to any its export does not account for. A main page keeps its XOR ID, which links in documents name; it is of the second shape, so it is covered too.
- **The CI runners on Shawn's computer held more than they let on.** A job could become root, read the runner's registration, leave files for later jobs (main's included), and reach the home network and the tailnet; their README said a job reached nothing of the computer. Each job now gets a fresh container, registered for that one job by a token outside it, with no sudo (Playwright's libraries are in the image), run by a user of its own, `mila-ci`, whose traffic a firewall keeps to the internet (retired 2026-11-15, when CI moved to GitHub's machines).
- **A reference in a comment could show a page's title and lead off-site.** A link was taken for one in the app by its path and query alone, so `//evil.example/wiki?page=<id>` (or `https://evil.example/...`, or with a backslash or a tab) showed as a reference to the page, under its title, and a click went to the other site; the server renamed such links with the page too. A link is now the app's only as a path in it or a full address of the app's own origin (the server's `MILA_BASE_URL`), as the browser would resolve it.
- **Presence could fill the memory.** One socket could claim any number of Yjs clients in a document's room, each a state kept and sent to everyone who opened the document after: twenty sockets made gigabytes in minutes. A socket now holds at most four clients in a room (a window has one per open document), and a room 200 states.
- **A small import could write hundreds of megabytes of files.** An import's files were limited to twice the largest upload allowed, not twice the upload, and the disk's free space was not checked as they were written, so a 500 KB zip of files of zeros wrote 512 MB, and again with each import. They are now limited by the upload's own size, and the disk is checked before each file.
- **Exports could fill the disk.** Each JSON export copies every file of the workspace and is kept a week, and the only limit was three on the way at once, so one person could make a new copy every few minutes. One person now keeps ten at most (deleting one makes room), starts ten an hour, and none starts or copies another file while the disk is low.
- **Writes had no quota, and page history no end.** Sync took any amount from anyone who could write, guests included, and kept every version of a page for good, so pushing a page of a million characters again and again grew the database without end. What one person writes through sync and REST together is now budgeted in bytes, 32 MB at once and then 32 MB an hour (`httpapi/writebudget.go`); while the database's disk is low only small writes are taken. A push refused for either is refused whole: the app keeps its edits, says so once, and sends them when the server's Retry-After says. A page keeps its newest 500 versions, at most 50 MB of them.
- **Signing someone out everywhere left ways back in.** An admin's sign out everywhere ended sessions, tokens and feeds, but not an email change or sign-in link asked for before, nor a passkey added, so whoever took the account could move it to their own address or sign straight back in. It now ends those too and removes every passkey, and the person is emailed; and an email change works only while the session that asked for it lasts, so revoking a session in Settings, Security ends what it asked for.
- **A bare carriage return could reach SMTP.** Mail turned only line feeds into CRLF, and a task's title or a project's name could hold a carriage return in the middle, which notification emails quote, so a member could end a message early with `\r.\r\n` and a lenient relay would send what followed as a second message from Mila's address. Every line of a message now ends with CRLF whatever ended it, and one-line fields (titles and names) refuse line breaks; an export from before comes in with them made spaces, and the task panel's title joins lines pasted into it.
- **Renaming leaked hidden titles, and could fail on hidden documents.** A rename rewrote every document linking to the item, so a task in a project a guest is not given, linked from a page they read, showed them its new title; and a guest's rename was made as the guest, so it failed, saying so, when a document they cannot see linked to the item. Renames are now the server's own edit, and reach only the documents whose every reader sees what was renamed (`seenByReaders` in `syncer/links.go`); elsewhere the link keeps its old words.
- **A guest's refused write could describe hidden tasks.** Sync checked what a row names (that its status is the project's, that its parent is in the same project, that a dependency makes no loop) before checking that a guest may write it, so a guest who knew a hidden task's ID (task history shows a dependency's blocker) got its title in the loop message, and the other messages answered whether an ID was a status or task elsewhere. Who may write a row is now checked first, and to a guest a row they cannot see answers as one that does not exist.
- **Switching accounts kept the first person's copy.** A sign-in link for another account switched the session but kept the device's copy, which is one database for whoever is signed in: the second person started on the first one's workspaces, private rows and projects they cannot see included, and the first one's unsent edits were sent as the second's, from that tab or any other still open. The device now records whose copy it holds (`mila.owner`, `claimDevice` in `signout.ts`): the app deletes another person's copy, as signing out does, before it starts, other tabs start again when it changes, and a push names whom its edits were made as, so the server refuses one for someone else (409 `other_user`). The switch page says how many unsent changes switching deletes.
- **Release builds restored caches that runs on main write.** A version tag's desktop build restored the cargo registry and `target/`, and its image build the Actions cache, so whatever a cache held went into the installers and the release image unchecked. Tag builds now restore no cache (Go, npm, cargo or Docker layers), and no build caches `target/`.
- **The database was readable by every user of the computer** in a bare-binary install: the systemd unit set no umask or state directory mode, and SQLite makes its files as the umask allows. The unit now keeps the data directory at 0700 with a umask of 0077, adds the usual sandboxing (no devices, kernel or clock changes, a system-call filter, no writable and executable memory), and Mila makes the database, its log and its memory file readable by their owner only as it opens them, so an install from before is put right at its next start.
- **The smaller ones: signing in.** Making someone an instance admin needs a recent sign-in. A sign-in link signs in as it opens only in the browser that asked for it in the last 15 minutes; anywhere else it says whose account it is and waits for a click, so a link someone sends cannot sign a person in as its sender, and a mail scanner that runs script spends nothing. Asking for a link for an address with no account does the same work as for one, so the time taken tells nothing. A signed-in person accepting an invitation for another address joins as a member or guest only: an admin's or owner's invitation is for the address it was sent to. Mila listens on `127.0.0.1:8080` unless told otherwise (the image still listens on `:8080` inside its container).
- **The smaller ones: who sees what.** Removing a member, or deleting a workspace, takes their open sockets out of its documents at once, and a device in a document's room is asked again every 30 seconds whether it may see it. Page history names a guest's fellow guests they share no project with as Someone, as task history does. A person's exports of a workspace go when they leave it, become a guest, or lose a project as a guest. A notification about a task or thread that is gone for good is no longer shown, rather than shown to anyone in the workspace. A guest is told what an automation did, not what it is called, in notifications and their export's history. A batch ID counts once for its sender, in its workspace, for ten minutes. A Yjs update of more than 100,000 structs and delete ranges is refused before it is built (`ydoc/parts.go`), since each costs a hundred times its bytes in memory. The 20 sockets a person may hold are counted as each is taken, so upgrades arriving together cannot pass it.
- **The smaller ones: the editor.** A link's class, target and rel are drawn as the app's own, whatever a hand-made update put in the document, and a table's column widths as whole numbers. A page fills from a template only when this tab made it for that template, not whenever its address names one. The font check uses `Object.hasOwn`. Signing out removes everything else Mila kept in the browser: preferences, themes, the workspace's theme, layouts.
- **The smaller ones: mail in and out.** Without a DMARC verdict, mail that fails SPF, softly or not, counts as forged unless a DKIM signature of the From address's own domain passed: anyone can sign with a domain of their own. A workspace's webhooks make 2000 tries an hour at most (a delivery past that waits), and test deliveries are limited per person. A plain `smtp://` server that offers no STARTTLS is refused, unless it is on this computer or the URL says `?tls=none`. A token may not be named email or after an automation, which history shows its changes beside.
- **The smaller ones: the desktop app and deploying.** A place's page may take a system-wide shortcut only of two modifiers and a letter, digit or Space, never one other programs hold (a terminal's copy and paste, opening a terminal, locking the screen); it may open the browser five times a minute at most; and its notifications name the place they come from. In local mode the session cookie's name carries the port, so other servers on this computer do not share it. The sign-in listener reads each connection on a thread of its own. Third-party actions and the image's base images are pinned by digest, which Renovate keeps current; govulncheck is pinned. A release keeps the installers it has (no `--clobber`) and has their checksums beside them; they are not signed. `compose.proxy.yaml` trusts only its own network's gateway rather than every Docker network. `main` is not protected yet: that is a setting of the repository.

### Security review (2026-11-15)

Before the first public release, seven read-only reviews of what changed since the review of 2026-11-08 (`1b8af58..dcfcb43`: agent access and OAuth; accounts and mail; who sees the weekly report and time on projects; the web app; deploying, the release and CI; the desktop app) and of what a public repository opens (its history, releases and workflows). One high finding, a few medium, no critical. What they found, and what changed:

- **A fork's pull request could run on Shawn's computer.** The self-hosted runners were the repository's, and a pull request brings its own workflow files, so it picks its runner. CI now runs on GitHub's machines (section 13), pull requests get a read-only token and no secrets, and the runners are retired. Mila goes public as a fresh repository, so nothing of the old one's runs, caches or releases comes along.
- **The install script could be told to skip its check.** It decided whether to check the attestation, and against which tag, from a name in the release's own `SHA256SUMS`. Now from `--version` or GitHub's latest tag; section 8 has the rest. The release job makes a release with its files, since an immutable release takes none after.
- **A guest could still steer `mila queue`** through a subtask or a comment made while the job ran. The queue checks every subtask's history and the people assigned, refuses a history cut at its limit, and checks again before it answers.
- **A hostile server's page might reach the desktop app's Places screen** for a moment on Linux. Only the app itself goes there now, its commands refuse while a place is on show, and deleting a place's work asks in the system's own dialog (section 3, the desktop app).
- **The Microsoft sign-in's key could be lost for good** by a process with the wrong data directory, `mila config check` included; now it pauses and resumes when the key is back (section 11). Sealing protects backups made after it only: a sign-in from before 3.0 is in plain text in older backups, which want deleting, or the account's sessions revoking at Microsoft.
- **Smaller ones.** A guest's REST list of dependencies held ones to tasks they can't see (now both tasks' projects, for any row in two). History gave a hidden guest's user ID. The server's CSV export let a formula through after a `;` (both writers now follow `shared/csv-vectors.json`). Behind a proxy that adds its own `X-Forwarded-For` line a client could pick its address. The weekly report ran in the loop that runs reminders (now a job each). `find-tasks` paged by OFFSET (now by place, bounded). The OAuth registry could be filled from a few dozen addresses, and any bare redirect scheme passed (now networks are counted, and schemes are reversed domains or a few known clients'). Importers had patterns that took the square of a hostile file's length, and Notion's zip could inflate a thousandfold. Slugs could cut a character, names could hold invisible characters, and `mila api` printed C1 controls.
- **Left as they are:** the web push key and webhook secrets are kept in plain text in the database, as before; sealing them would make a restore without `secret.key` break every push subscription and webhook signature.

## 11. Integrations and API

### One API, defined first

- The API is described in `shared/openapi.yaml`. Typed Go server code and a typed TypeScript client are generated from it, so they cannot drift.
- REST under `/api/v1`, through the same domain layer and `can()` as sync.
- The app uses REST for everything that is not sync: sign-in, invites, uploads, exports, admin.
- Read and write endpoints for every entity exist from day one, so a public API later only needs tokens.
- Interactive reference at `/api/docs`.
- (Changed before M7: a person's themes are `GET /me/themes`, and `GET`, `PUT` and `DELETE /me/themes/{themeId}`, under the `me` tag. A PUT is checked strictly against the registry (unknown tokens, values out of bounds or of the wrong kind, and refs to a token of the same or a higher layer are refused), under the user's lock so the 20-theme cap holds against racing saves; a whole theme is last writer wins, and an edit arriving after a delete makes it again. The WebSocket sends `{"type":"themes","id":...}` to the person's other devices, which fetch that one theme; older apps ignore the type.)

(Changed before M7: built. Every synced entity has list, get, create (not attachments, which only an upload makes), change (not a task's label or assignee, which are only added and removed) and delete under `/api/v1/workspaces/{workspaceId}/<collection>`, kebab-case plurals such as `tasks` and `custom-field-values`. Rows are flat JSON with the snake_case names sync uses, plus read-only `id`, `created_at` and `updated_at`. They are row-level only: the app's compound actions (a new project's statuses, a status kept in step with completion, the next task of a series, subtasks moving with their parent, re-homing a deleted status's tasks, relative reminders following a due date) stay in `data.ts`, and the spec's description says how to do each by hand. Each write is one sync op through the merge (section 7), with the same permissions through `authz`, guests and private rows; another person's private row is a 404 through REST, stricter than sync. Fields are last writer wins; `If-Match` with an ETag (a hash of the row's clocks) is optional and answers 412 when the row changed. An `Idempotency-Key` on a POST makes a retried create name the same row and a retry a duplicate, kept as long as applied ops are (90 days); the row a retry answers with is checked for sight again, so someone who has since lost it gets 404. If-Match and If-None-Match take `*` and lists, If-None-Match comparing weakly as RFC 9110 says. A field that names its maker (an author, the owner of a My Day entry) is refused with 403 when it names anyone else, before a pair's ID is made from it. A pair's POST (a label on a task, an assignee, a My Day entry, a field value) derives its ID from the pair and answers 200 with the row when it exists, bringing back a deleted one. A create with no `sort_key` goes after the largest key of its kind in the workspace, through a Go port of `sortkey.ts` held to `shared/sortkey-vectors.json`. Lists come a page at a time in ID order, filtered in the SQL by who may see what, with an equality filter for every reference, person, choice, flag and date field. A script signs in with the session cookie and sends `Origin`, as the app does, until tokens. The endpoints and their schemas are generated: `httpapi/restgen` writes the marked regions of `shared/openapi.yaml` and the one-line handlers in `httpapi/rows_gen.go` from the entity registry, so a new entity or field gets its endpoints from `make generate`. The attachment download moved to `/attachments/{id}/file`, since the row now lives at `/attachments/{id}`. The reference at `/api/docs` is a small page of the app's own, readable signed out, which reads the spec as JSON from `/api/v1/openapi.json` (embedded in the binary rather than in `web/static`, which the service worker would download to every device) and lets someone signed in try any request as themselves; Settings, Your data links to it.) (Changed before M7, after review: the JSON fields have shapes the server checks on every write, by sync and REST alike, since a value the app could not read broke the page for everyone who opened it: a view's config is an object whose filter, when present, has each part of its JSON type, its display an object or null, a field's choices a list of {id, name, color} with ids unique and colors of the palette, and a field value the JSON type its field's type gives it. Unknown keys are kept for newer apps. Their byte limits count the compact JSON. Every sort_key written must be one sortkey.ts could make, and a create without one goes after the largest valid key. Only a write that sets a subtask's project or parent is held to its parent's project, so a parent moved by REST leaves its subtasks editable where they are until each is moved too; the task schema says so. The app reads what was stored before defensively: a malformed filter part, or a config or filter that is not an object, matches nothing, on the server's feeds as in the app.)

## 12. Data ownership

### Export

Runs as a background job; you get a download link.

- **JSON:** lossless, versioned, complete, attachments bundled in a zip. Restores into any instance.
- **CSV per project:** flattened, one column per custom field.
- **Markdown:** a folder per project, a file per task, fields in frontmatter, comments below the notes. Readable in Obsidian or any editor.

(Changed in M6: an export is of one workspace, as the person asking sees it: the rows their sync would send, plus deleted ones still in the trash, and the history of those tasks. Every member may export, guests too; a guest's export holds only the projects shared with them and no one's email address, since the member list is not theirs to see, and a member's holds the addresses of current members only, as the member list shows them; people who have left are named without one. It is a job in the slow lane (section 7) that writes a zip to `<data_dir>/exports`, outside the attachments folder, whose sweep would delete it. When it is ready the person gets an in-app notification that leads to Settings, Your data, where it downloads for 7 days by its maker only; then it expires, row and file. One person may have three on the way at once, and ten kept (on the way or ready; deleting one makes room), and start ten an hour; none starts, and none copies another file, while the disk is low, as uploads are refused. A failed export is not retried by the runner: the list says why, and asking again is one click. (Changed before M7: exports leave people's pictures out, and the format version and the importer are unchanged: an import credits everything to the person importing and keeps no one else, so pictures would have no use there.)

The JSON zip holds `manifest.json` (format `mila-export`, version 1, the schema version, who exported it and when, counts), `workspace.json` (every row with its fields and clocks, in sync order, the history, and the people they name) and `attachments/<id>/<file name>`. The CSV zip holds one file per project and `Inbox.csv`, a row per task (subtasks too, naming their parent), with custom field choices and people by name; a cell that starts like a formula, heading cells (custom field names) included, gets a leading apostrophe so a spreadsheet does not run it. The Markdown zip holds a folder per project and `Inbox`, file and folder names made safe for Windows, frontmatter as double-quoted YAML, subtasks linked to their parent with `[[...]]`, and the checklist, the attachments by name, and the comments with their author and time below the notes; the files themselves are only in the JSON export.)

### Import

- JSON restores into a new workspace, keeping original IDs or assigning fresh ones.
- CSV goes through the mapping screen, always with a dry run first.

(Changed in M6: a JSON import is a job in the slow lane. The upload, up to `MILA_IMPORT_MAX_MB` (512 by default; 256 since the M7 security review), waits in `<data_dir>/imports` and is checked at once (a zip, with a Mila manifest of a version this Mila reads). The job makes a workspace owned by the importer and keeps it out of sight (its `deleted_at` set) until the import is complete, so nobody sees half of one; a failed import removes it. Every row goes through the same field checks as a sync op, and rows that do not fit are left out and counted rather than failing the import; a reference to something the export did not hold is cleared where that changes no one's view of the row, and the row is left out otherwise. Keeping IDs is refused if any of them is already on the instance (the original is still there); fresh IDs remake the IDs the app derives from others (a pair's row, the next task of a series, its copied checklist and reminders) the way the app makes them, and rewrite IDs held inside a view's filter. The person who made the export becomes the importer, so their My Day, reminders, personal views and assignments come with it; other people's private rows and assignments are left out, since only the importer is a member, and the rest of other people's work (their comments, files, tasks they created and history) is credited to the importer. Nothing in an export proves who wrote what, since anyone can make one, so keeping the names would let an importer put words in someone's mouth and, as owner of the result, read their email address in its export. Work by a deleted account stays theirs, shown as "Deleted user", which tells nobody anything. Attachments come in as new files, together at most twice the upload's size (an export stores its files as they are, so its own never come near it), and only while the disk has the room an upload needs. One import at a time per person; a report says what came in and what was left out, and the importer is told in the app when it is done. What an import reads is bounded by its upload, not only by fixed limits, so a small zip cannot unpack into gigabytes: the zip's directory is counted before it is opened (200,000 entries at most), and `workspace.json` is read a row at a time, refusing more rows and history entries than one per 16 bytes of upload (never fewer than 10,000, never more than 2 million; Mila's own exports zip a row into about 95 bytes). An import the server stops under is queued again for after the restart; one the server died under (it may be what killed it) fails rather than being tried again.

CSV import runs in the browser, as ordinary edits through the sync engine, so it works offline and passes the same checks as typing; there is no server endpoint for it. The mapping screen guesses each column from its heading (the usual names, Mila's own CSV export, custom fields by name) and the person can change any. Rows with no project column go to a new project named after the file, an existing project or the inbox. The dry run is required, and is made again when a choice changes: it lists the tasks that would come in, the rows left out (no title) and the values that could not be read, and every project, status, label and field choice it would make. Dates are read as 2026-11-03; a status named like done or canceled completes the task. An import takes 5000 rows at most. Its edits are not on the undo list, which holds far fewer; the finished import offers to move its tasks to the trash instead.)

(Deferred in M6: `mila export` and `mila import` on the command line, since backups and `migrate-db` cover moving a whole instance; an export of every workspace at once; keeping imported work's original authors as people of their own, which needs a way to map and invite them and a way to trust the file (a signed export, say); importing Mila's CSV or Markdown exports as round trips, since only JSON is promised to come back whole; importers for other apps, which section 11 already puts after v1.)

### Backups (instance level)

- Nightly SQLite `VACUUM INTO`, or `pg_dump` for Postgres.
- Retention: 7 daily, 4 weekly, 6 monthly.
- Local directory plus optional S3-compatible storage; off-site copies encrypted with `age`.
- `mila backup now`, `mila backup list`, `mila restore <file>`.
- The admin page shows the last successful backup; a failure sends an email.

(Changed in M6: a backup is always a SQLite file, whichever database the instance runs on. SQLite is copied with `VACUUM INTO`; Postgres is read in one repeatable-read transaction and copied into a new SQLite file by the copier `migrate-db` uses, rather than by `pg_dump`. The Docker image is distroless and holds no `pg_dump`, a `pg_dump` must match the server's major version, and one file format means one restore path for both databases. Backups go to `MILA_BACKUP_DIR` as `mila-<time>-<why>-s<schema>.db`, readable by the owner only (they hold sign-in secrets), and are named so a listing needs no file opened. The why is `nightly`, `manual` (`mila backup now`, or the admin page), `premigrate` (before migrations, section 8) or `prerestore`.

The nightly one is due from `MILA_BACKUP_HOUR`, UTC, and is queued as a slow job once per night; the night last queued is stored, so a restart neither skips one nor makes two, and a server that was off makes one when it is back. A new instance makes its first the next night. Retention counts the nightly and manual ones together and keeps the newest of each of the last 7 days that have one, of the last 4 weeks and of the last 6 months; the last 3 before migrations and the last 3 before restores are kept apart from those. `Retain` is a pure function with table tests.

The status is in `instance_setting` (`backup.last_ok_at`, `backup.last_error`, `backup.last_error_at`) for the admin page, with the list of backups. The first failure after a success emails every instance admin, through the mail lane; later failures in the same run only update the status, and the next success ends the run.

`mila restore <file>` (a path, or a name from `mila backup list`) needs the server stopped. It backs up the database as it is first, refuses a backup from a newer Mila and migrates an older one, works on a copy so the backup itself never changes, then swaps the SQLite file (and removes the old write-ahead log) or replaces every row of the Postgres database in one transaction. It writes a new sync epoch (section 7), so every device bootstraps again and keeps the edits it had not sent.

Deferred in M6: off-site copies to S3-compatible storage, encrypted with `age`, wait for Shawn's choice of storage (section 15), since a Hetzner Storage Box speaks no S3 and the answer decides what to build. Until then the backups stay on the server's disk, and attachments are not in them: the attachments folder (`<data dir>/attachments`) needs its own backup, for example by copying the whole data directory elsewhere. `mila config check` testing SMTP and S3 waits with it.) (Decided for M7: off-site copies are put off past M7. Production runs on Shawn's NAS, so the NAS cannot be its own off-site copy, and the NAS has no off-site backup yet; the likely answer is age-encrypted backups, attachments included, in a NAS folder that a backup of the NAS carries away. Until then M7's "backed up" means the nightly local backups.) (Decided 2026-10-31: no off-site copies. Shawn dropped them; the nightly local backups are the backups.) (Changed before M7: people's pictures live in the same folder, so the same holds for them: backups and `migrate-db` carry `users.avatar`, but after a restore onto a fresh data directory a picture whose file is missing shows initials.)

### Switching databases

- `mila migrate-db --to postgres://…` (and the reverse) copies the whole instance. Start on SQLite and move later with one command.

(M6: it needs the server stopped, backs up and migrates the database in use, migrates the new one, which must be empty, and copies every table in one transaction, then prints the URL to set. The copier (`db.Copy`) reads the tables, columns and references from the databases themselves, so a table added later is copied without a list to update; it writes tables after the ones they reference and subtasks after their parents, and converts what SQLite keeps as integers and text into Postgres booleans and times, and back. A round-trip test fills every table, copies SQLite to Postgres and back, and compares every value. The old database is left as it was, and devices carry on, since the copy is exact.)

### Retention

- Trash: deleted items restorable for 30 days, then purged.
- Activity history: kept forever.
- Sync log: compacted after 30 days.
- Account deletion: the personal workspace is removed; content in shared workspaces stays, credited to "Deleted user".

(Changed in M6: deleting an account is in Settings, Your data, and asks for the person's email address to be typed. It removes, for good, the personal workspace and every shared workspace where the person is the only member. It is refused, naming the workspaces, while they are the only owner of one that others still use: ownership moves first, since deleting other people's work, or leaving it with no owner, is not theirs to decide. It is refused too for the instance's only admin. In the shared workspaces left behind, their assignments end through sync, so every device drops them, and their My Day, reminders and personal views go. The user row stays, emptied: named "Deleted user", its email replaced by an address that cannot receive mail, its settings cleared, `deleted_at` set. Deleting the row would delete their comments and files with it (those references cascade) and fail on the invitations they sent, and the app shows "Deleted user" wherever their work is. Their sessions, passkeys, sign-in links, memberships, guest access, notifications, calendar feeds, push subscriptions, exports, imports, security log, known devices and custom themes (`user_themes`, since the work before M7) are deleted, and every open device of theirs is signed out. Backups made before the deletion still hold their data until those backups expire under the retention above. (Changed before M7: their picture is cleared too, and its file swept within the hour.) Deferred in M6: asking for a fresh sign-in before deleting, beyond the typed address; for the M7 security review.)
